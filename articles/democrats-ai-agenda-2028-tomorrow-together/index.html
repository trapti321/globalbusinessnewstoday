Democratic Group Tomorrow Together Launches $1.1 Million Push to Shape AI Agenda for 2028

Tomorrow Together, which spent $6.5M on pro-regulation midterm candidates, launches a $1.1M AI research push for 2028. What it means for tech.

GBNT Newsroom • Published Nov 13, 2026 • 5 min read
Share

Tomorrow Together, a Democratic political organization that spent $6.5 million backing pro-regulation candidates in the 2026 midterms, has launched a $1.1 million research initiative to shape how Democrats talk to voters about artificial intelligence ahead of the 2028 presidential election, The Associated Press reported on November 13, 2026. The group says AI regulation is now a winning issue for Democrats, setting up a sharper political fight with an AI industry that has spent tens of millions of dollars on elections.

Democrats' AI push, the key facts:

  • Who: Tomorrow Together, co-founded by Karthik Ganapathy and Rob Flaherty. Maddie Kriger is chief program officer.
  • What: a research initiative starting at $1.1 million on how Democrats should discuss AI with voters.
  • Midterm record: $6.5 million spent supporting candidates who called for more AI regulation and new limits on data centers.
  • Where announced: the What's Next conference in Denver, where Democrats met after the midterms to plan for 2028.
  • The divide: President Trump opposes regulating powerful AI; Democrats argue that stance is unpopular.

What Is Tomorrow Together Launching?

Tomorrow Together is starting a research effort, initially funded at $1.1 million, to work out how Democrats should talk about AI with voters before the 2028 race, according to the AP report by Nicholas Riccardi, also published by US News. The group announced it as Democrats gathered in Denver for What's Next, a two-day conference marking their midterm gains and looking toward the next presidential campaign.

"We expect getting this right is going to be critical to winning people's votes," Kriger told the AP. She added: "It's also not out of the realm of possibility we will see more and worse AI incidents in the next year."

The AP did not describe the group's legal structure or say who funds the new initiative. Some early versions of the wire story misnamed the group "Together Tomorrow"; the AP corrected it to Tomorrow Together.

Why Democrats See AI as a Winning Issue

The group's founders argue that voters have moved toward regulation. "At the beginning of the cycle, this was a jump ball. Voters didn't know who to trust," Flaherty said. "Regulating technology is where the voters are." Ganapathy, speaking about Republicans and AI, said: "I'd rather be us than them."

Democratic data analyst David Shor told the AP: "I really think we're watching the birth of a new political issue before our eyes."

Public opinion data supports the idea that AI worries cross party lines. A Pew Research Center survey of 5,023 US adults in June 2025 found 50% were more concerned than excited about AI in daily life, up from 37% in 2021. Republicans (50%) and Democrats (51%) were almost identical. Trust in Washington to regulate AI differs by party: in a March 2025 Pew survey, 54% of Republicans trusted the US to regulate AI effectively, against 36% of Democrats.

How Much Is the AI Industry Spending on Elections?

Tomorrow Together's $6.5 million was a fraction of what AI-aligned super PACs spent in the midterm cycle. According to an October 29 analysis by The Intercept:

  • Leading the Future, an industry-backed network launched in August 2025, counts OpenAI president Greg Brockman and his wife, Anna Brockman, among its donors, with a $25 million gift. It had about $34 million in cash across its network at its latest report.
  • Public First, a rival network formed to push for tighter AI rules, received $1 million from Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei.
  • The two networks spent $50.7 million in primaries, including $21.3 million for and against New York Democrat Alex Bores, a state lawmaker who sponsored AI safety legislation, in a single congressional primary.

The Intercept found only about 5% of AI super PAC direct spending went to contested general-election races, with most money spent in primaries.

Where Republicans and the Trump Administration Stand

The AP reports that President Donald Trump opposes regulating powerful AI, a position Democrats argue is unpopular with voters and see as an opening to lead on AI guardrails. Not every Republican agrees with the White House. Florida Gov. Ron DeSantis, widely expected to consider another presidential run, has distanced himself from Trump's pro-industry stance, according to the AP.

Democrats are not fully united either: some party leaders have courted AI investment and data center projects in their states, while others want tougher limits. How the party balances jobs and investment against regulation will shape its 2028 platform.

What It Means for Business

For technology companies and investors, the push signals that AI regulation is likely to be a central 2028 campaign issue rather than a niche policy debate. Areas most exposed include:

  • Data centers: Tomorrow Together's midterm candidates sought new limits on data centers, which have drawn protests, including an anti-data center demonstration at the Pennsylvania state Capitol on October 6, 2026, pictured in AP coverage.
  • AI safety rules: requirements for testing and disclosure of the most powerful models, especially as companies develop AI that can help build newer versions of itself, a risk the AP said prompted the new initiative.
  • Campaign finance: industry spending through super PACs may itself become a campaign issue. A Data for Progress poll commissioned by Guardrails Alliance found about three-quarters of likely voters in Iowa, Michigan and Ohio were less likely to back a candidate supported by an AI super PAC, The Intercept reported.

Businesses deploying AI should watch state legislatures, where most AI rules are currently being written, and the policy platforms that emerge from 2028 primary candidates.
