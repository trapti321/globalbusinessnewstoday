Meta Tests Takes, a Prediction Market App That Uses Points Instead of Cash, With Employees

Meta is testing Takes, a prediction market app that uses "cred" points instead of money, with employees, WSJ reported. No public launch date is set.

GBNT Newsroom • Published Nov 16, 2026 • 5 min read
Share

Meta Platforms has started testing a prediction market app called Takes with its own employees, The Wall Street Journal reported on Friday, November 13, 2026. Unlike Kalshi and Polymarket, the app lets users bet points called "cred" instead of real money. Meta declined to comment and has not said whether or when the app will reach the public.

Meta's Takes prediction app, the key facts:

  • What it is: A standalone app, previously called Arena, where users predict the outcomes of real-world events.
  • Money: No cash. Users win "cred" points for correct predictions and lose them for wrong ones.
  • Who can use it: Only Meta employees, in an internal test. No public launch date or country rollout has been announced.
  • Who reported it: The Wall Street Journal broke the test on November 13. The New York Times first reported the Arena project on June 23, 2026.
  • Meta's response: Meta declined to comment and gave no timetable for a public release.
  • The rivals: Kalshi was valued at $40 billion in October; Polymarket at $21 billion in August.

What Is Meta Testing?

According to the Wall Street Journal, which cited people familiar with the matter, Meta employees are now using an unreleased app called Takes to forecast events. Users earn cred when they are right and lose it when they are wrong, The Block reported, summarizing the Journal's story.

Employees have been asked whether former Apple CEO Tim Cook will become Nvidia's next chief executive, Investing.com reported, and on November 13 staff were asked to pick the winner of Jeopardy!, according to 24/7 Wall St.

The Journal's report did not say whether real-money contracts remain an option for later. Meta declined to comment.

Where Did the Idea Come From?

The project first surfaced in June. NPR reported on June 24 that CEO Mark Zuckerberg had told a small team to build the app, then called Arena, after The New York Times first reported the plan on June 23. NPR said internal documents it obtained referred to the project by the codenames "Antwerp" and "FBForecast."

Those documents described a daily allotment of play money for users, with Meta's Llama AI model generating questions from trending topics, making "personalized market recommendations" and resolving markets in "near real-time." The plan called for employee testing before any public release on iPhone and Android, which is the stage Takes has now reached.

It is not Meta's first try. The company launched a crowdsourced prediction app called Forecast in 2020 and shut it down about two years later. NPR said the new documents describe the project as a "rebuild" of Forecast and cite "the operational cost of manual question curation" as the reason the original was closed.

Why Does Skipping Real Money Matter?

Real-money prediction markets are in the middle of a legal fight between federal and state regulators. Kalshi and Polymarket face state lawsuits alleging illegal gambling, while the platforms argue their event contracts are legal and fall under federal jurisdiction, The Block reported.

The Commodity Futures Trading Commission (CFTC), which oversees these exchanges, has sided with the industry. On April 2, 2026, the agency sued Illinois, Arizona and Connecticut over their efforts to police prediction markets, The Block reported. Illinois had sent cease-and-desist letters to Kalshi, Crypto.com and Polymarket. "The CFTC will continue to safeguard its exclusive regulatory authority over these markets," CFTC Chairman Michael Selig said at the time.

The courts are split. Two federal appeals courts ruled in August and September that states can regulate sports-related event contracts, while a third sided with Kalshi in April, and the dispute will likely end up at the Supreme Court, CNBC reported. Speaking at the Cantor Convergence conference this week, Selig criticized states that have pursued criminal charges against prediction market executives. "That's not something that should be at the risk of going to jail," he said, according to The Block.

Polymarket's figures come from Bloomberg News, citing people familiar with the matter. Meta's draw is reach: more than 3 billion people use at least one of its apps daily, NPR noted, though the company has struggled to make new standalone apps stick.

What Happens Next?

Takes remains an internal test, and Meta has not committed to a public launch, a launch market or a real-money version. The bigger regulatory question, whether states or the CFTC control event contracts, is heading toward the Supreme Court, and its answer will shape whether a points-only app is Meta's final product or a first step.

FAQs

Does Meta's Takes app use real money?

No. According to The Wall Street Journal, Takes uses points called "cred," which users gain for correct predictions and lose for incorrect ones.

Can the public download Meta's prediction market app?

No. As of November 16, 2026, Takes is being tested only by Meta employees. Meta has not announced a launch date or the countries where it might be offered.

Who first reported Meta's prediction market plans?

The New York Times first reported Meta's Arena project on June 23, 2026. The Wall Street Journal first reported the employee test of the renamed Takes app on November 13, 2026.

Who regulates prediction markets in the US?

The CFTC oversees exchanges such as Kalshi, but several states argue the contracts are illegal gambling under state law. The CFTC has sued Illinois, Arizona and Connecticut over the issue, and conflicting appeals court rulings may send the dispute to the Supreme Court.
